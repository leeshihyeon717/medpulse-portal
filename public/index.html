Pharmacy

Pharmacists are medication experts who make sure patients get the safest, most effective drug therapy possible - whether that means filling a prescription, catching a dangerous drug interaction, or counseling a patient on how to take their medication correctly.

What Does a Pharmacist Do?

Pharmacists review and dispense prescriptions, verify correct dosages, screen for interactions between a patient's medications, and counsel patients on proper use and side effects. Many also administer vaccinations, manage chronic disease programs, and consult directly with physicians on treatment plans. Pharmacists work in retail and hospital pharmacies, long-term care facilities, clinical research, and the pharmaceutical industry.

Education & Training

Becoming a pharmacist requires a Doctor of Pharmacy (PharmD) degree, typically a four-year program following at least two to three years of undergraduate coursework in subjects like biology, chemistry, and physics. PharmD programs combine classroom instruction with supervised clinical rotations. Graduates must then pass the North American Pharmacist Licensure Examination (NAPLEX) and a state-specific law exam to practice in Pennsylvania.

Skills & Experience

Strong pharmacy candidates build a foundation in chemistry and biology, develop careful attention to detail, and gain comfort communicating with patients about sensitive health information. Experience working or volunteering in a pharmacy setting - even a retail one - is one of the most valuable things a student can do before applying to a PharmD program.

Career Pathway

Most students complete relevant undergraduate prerequisites, take the Pharmacy College Admission Test (PCAT) if required by their target programs, and apply to PharmD programs through PharmCAS. After graduation and licensure, many new pharmacists complete an optional one- or two-year residency to specialize in areas like oncology, critical care, or ambulatory care.

What Students Should Know

  • Pharmacy is a doctoral-level profession - the PharmD is required to practice.
  • Direct patient interaction is a daily part of the job, not just chemistry and counting pills.
  • Board certification in specialties (like oncology or psychiatric pharmacy) can open further career paths.

Nursing

Nurses provide the majority of hands-on patient care in the healthcare system - monitoring patients, administering treatment, educating families, and coordinating with the rest of the care team across nearly every clinical setting.

What Does a Nurse Do?

Registered nurses assess patient conditions, administer medications and treatments, monitor vital signs, and communicate changes in a patient's status to physicians. They educate patients and families about health conditions and discharge care, and coordinate with pharmacists, physical therapists, and other specialists. Nurses work in hospitals, clinics, schools, long-term care facilities, and community health settings.

Education & Training

Registered nurses typically complete either an Associate Degree in Nursing (ADN) or a Bachelor of Science in Nursing (BSN), both of which include supervised clinical rotations across specialties like pediatrics, surgery, and mental health. Graduates must pass the National Council Licensure Examination (NCLEX-RN) to become licensed. A BSN is increasingly preferred by hospitals and opens more advanced career paths.

Skills & Experience

Nursing demands clinical judgment under pressure, clear communication with patients and families, physical stamina, and comfort working as part of a team. Volunteering at a hospital or long-term care facility, becoming a Certified Nursing Assistant (CNA), or shadowing a working nurse are strong ways to confirm interest before committing to a nursing program.

Career Pathway

After earning an RN license, nurses can pursue specialty certifications (such as critical care or oncology nursing), or continue on to a Master of Science in Nursing (MSN) to become a Nurse Practitioner, Nurse Educator, or Clinical Nurse Specialist - roles with greater autonomy and, in many states, prescriptive authority.

What Students Should Know

  • Nursing offers many entry points - ADN, BSN, and accelerated programs for career-changers all lead to RN licensure.
  • Shift work, including nights, weekends, and holidays, is common in hospital nursing.
  • Nursing has one of the widest ranges of specialties of any health profession.

Education Pathways

Every non-physician health career has its own combination of coursework, licensing exams, and clinical training. Here's a general overview of how those pathways compare - each career page linked below goes into full detail.

Pharmacy - Doctor of Pharmacy (PharmD)

2-3 years prerequisites + 4-year PharmD program + NAPLEX licensing exam.

Nursing - Registered Nurse (RN)

Associate or Bachelor's degree in nursing + clinical rotations + NCLEX-RN licensing exam.

Physical & Occupational Therapy

Doctoral-level programs (DPT / OTD) following a bachelor's degree, plus national board exams.

A General Timeline

Most non-physician health careers follow a similar arc: relevant high school coursework (biology, chemistry, anatomy where offered), an undergraduate degree with the right prerequisites, admission to an accredited professional program, and a licensing exam before entering practice. The length and structure of the professional program varies significantly by field, so it's worth researching your specific career of interest early.

Student Resources

Student Resources

Practical, concrete steps Pennsylvania students can take right now to explore a health career and build a stronger application.

Shadowing & Volunteering

Reach out to local pharmacies, hospitals, and clinics directly - many are willing to host students for a few hours of observation, especially if introduced through a school counselor or family connection. Hospital volunteer programs are also a reliable way to get consistent, ongoing exposure to a clinical environment.

Extracurriculars & Clubs

HOSA (Health Occupations Students of America) chapters, school-based health science clubs, and science research programs all build relevant experience and connect you with peers considering similar paths. Local Area Health Education Center (AHEC) programs often run summer camps and pipeline programs specifically for students exploring health careers.

Ask a Professional

Every article on PA-NHCE includes a comment section where you can ask questions directly - our contributors and other visitors can respond. It's a low-pressure way to get real answers before reaching out to a professional directly.

About

About PA-NHCE

PA-NHCE is a student-facing resource dedicated to helping Pennsylvania students explore non-physician health careers - pharmacy, nursing, and beyond. We believe students make better decisions about their future when they have clear, accurate information about what these careers actually involve, written by people who understand the field.

Editorial Standards

All articles and medication information on PA-NHCE are written or reviewed by approved editorial contributors before publishing. We use role-based permissions so that medical article content and pharmacy/medication content are each maintained by contributors with the relevant background.

Approved Contributors

Verified contributors hold the credentials to publish, edit, or remove articles and medication guides within their assigned area.

Visitors & Students

Anyone can read articles, browse the medication directory, ask questions in the comments, and submit a site review. Editing published content is limited to approved contributors.

Disclaimer: Content on PA-NHCE is for educational purposes only. It does not constitute medical advice or a substitute for professional clinical judgment. Always consult a qualified healthcare provider regarding any medical condition.
